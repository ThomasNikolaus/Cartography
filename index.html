Homotopical Cartography Project

In this community project, we explore the landscape of mathematics through homotopical methods, using AI tools where they help. Our aim is to develop original mathematical insights, advance mathematical knowledge, and deepen human understanding, particularly in homotopy theory and neighbouring areas.

We present our findings in Exploration Charts: jointly authored mathematical writeups presenting results, examples, counterexamples, and proofs. Each Chart also records the approaches, limitations, difficulties, and open questions needed to understand and extend the work.

The Atlas

Together, the Charts form The Atlas: a growing collection of mathematical explorations, connected through common structures, methods, and questions.

Group completion commutes with infinite products

Georg Lehner, Thomas Nikolaus, Phil Pützstück

PDF

About this chart

2026-11-02 · Exploration Chart (not peer-reviewed)

We show that group completion of 𝔼ₙ-monoids commutes with infinite products for n ≥ 2, by reducing the question to a more general theorem about the infinite-product-preservation of the realization functor on a suitable class of simplicial 𝔼₁-monoids. We also provide an alternative shorter argument specifically for the case of 𝔼∞-monoids, which uses the known fact that K-theory of additive ∞-categories commutes with infinite products.

The main arguments have originally been generated through the use of generative AI, and then checked and simplified by the three authors. Building on this chart, we plan to write a fully-fledged article, that streamlines these arguments, makes them more conceptual, and presents them in a more reader-friendly form.

Some Exploration Charts may develop into full papers and be posted on arXiv or Hexagon; others may remain standalone explorations. Each Chart clearly states its status, what has been checked and understood by its human authors, and what remains provisional or open.

About the map

Urbano Monte drew this world map in Milan in 1587. Designed to form a planisphere about three metres in diameter, Monte’s sixty-sheet world map is one of the largest of the sixteenth century. In 2017, Brandon Rumsey digitally assembled the sheets into the continuous map Monte had envisaged. The illustrations shown here come from the David Rumsey Map Collection at Stanford University.
